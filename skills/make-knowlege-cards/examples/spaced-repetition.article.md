# Why cramming fails and what works instead

*Sample article written for this repository to demonstrate the skill.*

Most students prepare for an exam the same way: they read their notes again and
again on the last evening. It feels productive, and it often gets them through
the next morning. A month later, almost nothing is left.

The reason was described as early as 1885 by the German psychologist Hermann
Ebbinghaus. He memorised lists of meaningless syllables and measured how much he
could still recall later. The result is known as the forgetting curve: memory
loss is steepest in the first hours and days after learning and slows down
afterwards. Whoever learns something once and never returns to it loses most of
it quickly.

Ebbinghaus also found the remedy. Every time material is reviewed, it is
forgotten more slowly than before. This is why the same amount of study time
works better when it is spread out. Four sessions of 15 minutes over two weeks
leave more in memory than one full hour on the evening before the test.
Researchers call this the spacing effect. The gaps between sessions can grow as
the material becomes familiar.

How you review matters as much as when. Reading a page again makes it feel
familiar, and familiarity is easily mistaken for knowledge. What strengthens
memory is pulling the answer out of your own head, a finding known as the
testing effect. Closing the book and writing down everything you remember about
a chapter does more than reading the chapter a second time, even though it feels
harder and less pleasant.

A simple way to combine both ideas with paper flashcards is the Leitner system,
proposed by the German science journalist Sebastian Leitner in the 1970s. All
new cards start in box 1, which is reviewed every day. A card answered correctly
moves up one box; a card answered wrongly goes back to box 1. Higher boxes are
reviewed less often, for example every second, fourth and eighth day. Difficult
cards therefore come up often and easy ones rarely, without any planning.

Flashcard apps apply the same principle and calculate the intervals
automatically.

One last piece of advice concerns the cards themselves. A card that asks for
several things at once is hard to grade: if you remember two of three parts, was
the answer right? Such cards are better split, so that every card has exactly
one answer.
